# Pol’s LinkedIn posts — working doc

This document is a **writing and planning space** for posts published from Pol’s personal LinkedIn profile.

## Context
We want to reshape what people think Wingle is.

Right now, because of how we’ve been covered (and how we’ve shown up on socials), many people associate Wingle with **offline in-flight chat** — sometimes even dating — and assume it’s only useful on an airplane.

That’s part of the story, but it’s not the full picture.

**The mission hasn’t changed:** make travel better — before, during, and after the flight.
**What changed:** we built a broader product portfolio serving **two audiences**:

- **Travelers (B2C)**: the Wingle app helps you fly better without airline status. No subscription — credits when you need them.
- Travel professionals (B2B): two offers:
  - Trusted airport services partner (Wingle Pro): lounges + fast track, Meet & Assist, and VVIP experiences (where available) — confirmed per request and held to a quality bar. Agencies bring us their travelers; we confirm options and coordinate delivery for those travelers.
  - Interactive itineraries + reusable knowledge (Wingle Atlas): a product that moves beyond static PDFs; capture and reuse destination know‑how across the agency.

## Voice notes (how this should sound)
Based on our internal brand guidelines:
- Calm confidence, premium with warmth, concrete language.
- Avoid hype and absolutes (“guaranteed”, “instant”, “always”).
- When something depends on airport/timing, say it plainly: confirmed per request.
- Write like a builder: outcome first → how it works (briefly) → next step.
- Keep it “Pol format”: short lines, lots of breathing room.
- Light humor is ok (dry, quick): “Spoiler: …”, “Fast-forward…”, “Fair.”
- Prefer what we do > how we name it (names are optional, clarity is not).

## Defaults (locked for this batch)
- **Language**: EN.
- **Naming**: it’s ok to mention product names occasionally, but lead with **what we do**, not how we name it.
- **Inbound CTA**: DMs.
- **AI tooling**: we use multiple tools/models depending on the job (Cursor, Claude Code, Codex, ChatGPT, and many models via Cursor). No Lovable.
- **AI workflow**: agents are great for quick tests; for production we keep control — we adapt, refactor, and bring everything up to our standards. Context is key: we use the `/docs` folder to feed context and keep decisions consistent.

## Broad plan — repositioning on LinkedIn (personal)

### Objective
Make the “Wingle = offline chat” mental model feel incomplete — and replace it with:
> **Wingle = premium travel experiences + tools for travel professionals, built to translate complexity into calm.**

### Strategy (what we’ll repeat until it sticks)
- **Same mission**: make travel better.
- **Two audiences**: travelers + travel agencies.
- **Two offers for travel professionals** (no jargon): airport services partner + itineraries/knowledge product.
- **Operational credibility**: “clear steps, no guesswork”, “confirmed per request”, “quality bar”.
- **Builder POV**: how we design products, how we use AI internally, how we iterate fast (build → test → discard).

### Content pillars (rotate)
1) **Reframing**: mission + two audiences (kill the “chat-only” perception without being defensive).
2) **Airport experience**: what “premium” means operationally; why quality filtering matters.
3) **Itineraries + knowledge**: why PDFs fail; why “knowledge reuse” compounds.
4) **How we build**: ship → test → discard; what we learned, what we kept.
5) **AI internally (practical)**: where it helps, where humans stay accountable.
6) **Inbound hooks**: questions for travel pros, “send me a route”, lightweight calls to talk.

### Cadence (suggested)
- 2 posts/week for 6–8 weeks.
- Every 3–4 posts: a direct travel-pro CTA (DM me).

### Do / Don’t checklist
- Do: be specific, show constraints, show the steps.
- Don’t: oversell or make it a dramatic “pivot story”.
- Do: keep offline chat as part of the journey, but never as the headline.

### Post 1 — Wingle is not an offline chat app
For a long time, a lot of people associated Wingle with one thing: offline in‑flight chat.

Fair. That’s how many of you found us — and honestly, it was fun to build. 😉

And yes — some people even thought (and still think) we’re a dating app. Spoiler: No.

The chat was never the full point.

Wingle has always had one mission: make travel better. Not only during the flight, but before, during, and after.

Fast-forward to today, we serve two audiences:

For travelers: the Wingle app helps you fly better without airline status. No subscription. Use credits when you need them — the kind of upgrades that make the journey feel lighter (airport comfort, speed, fewer friction points).

For travel agencies: we support them in two ways:
- as a trusted partner for premium airport services (lounges + fast track, Meet & Assist, private terminals, etc.) — agencies bring us their travelers; we coordinate delivery
- with our most recent product, Wingle Atlas: interactive itineraries + reusable agency knowledge (so destination know‑how becomes searchable and shareable across the team)

If you’re a travel professional and you’re curious about either side, DM me. If you share a route + dates, I’ll tell you what “good” looks like.

### Post 2 — Premium at the airport isn’t a list. It’s a process.
Premium airport services are usually described like a menu: “fast track, lounge, meet & greet…”

But "premium" is having a great experience, and this comes with way more thought process than just selecting services.

Because what matters is not "getting all the services" - it is about getting the right ones:
- selecting the services that will add to the experience, discarding the ones that will not
- making sure they are up to standard
- getting clear instructions (so it’s actually easy to use once it’s booked)
- if context changes, handle it

And getting to that depends on a lot of variables in the airspace:
airport, timing, airline, connections, traveler profile, local providers, availability, quality.

So our value to agencies is not “a checklist" and some bookings.
It’s a curated, confirmed plan.
And the coordination behind it.

Behind the scenes, there’s a lot of internal knowledge and curation to get to the best end result.

We do use AI internally (more on this coming up), but for things that need to be accurate and account for a lot of variables, AI doesn’t get the final word.
Human judgment does.

And when that is done, the traveler feels looked after, everything is clear, and nothing “surprises” them at the airport.

If you run operations at a travel agency: what’s the biggest source of uncertainty in premium airport services today?

### Post 3 — The PDF itinerary problem
The real asset in a great travel agency isn’t the template.
It’s what your designers know.

The room to request.
The timing that avoids the queue.
The detail that makes a hotel feel personal.
The “this looks small but it changes the whole trip” kind of tip.

The problem is: this knowledge rarely becomes reusable.
It lives in people’s heads, in notes, in chats, in scattered docs.

That’s exactly why our most recent project is Wingle Atlas:
an internal knowledge layer + interactive itineraries.

And yes — part of why this matters is because the industry still delivers many trips in static PDFs.
Walls of text. Generic paragraphs. No context. No “what do I need right now?”

I’m not saying PDFs are evil.
They’re just not built for how people travel — and they’re definitely not built to retain agency knowledge.

If you’re a travel designer or agency lead: what’s your current system for “knowledge that should not get lost”?

### Post 4 — How we use AI internally (without the hype)
I’ve always been a bit of a hater of the “AI everywhere” era.

At some point, society started turning a shitty product into a “cool” one by just adding the word “AI” to it.
And I never bought it.

At Wingle, we use AI where it actually matters — and we avoid it where it doesn’t.

Some parts of travel (and building travel products) are messy by nature.
AI helps there.
And many parts require human touch, judgment, and responsibility.
AI doesn’t help there (at least not in the way people want it to).

Also: we don’t use “one AI”.
We use whatever fits the job: Cursor, Claude Code, Codex, ChatGPT, and a bunch of models through Cursor.
(No Lovable though — I like having more control.)

What AI is genuinely good at for us:
- turning unstructured inputs into something structured
- summarizing long threads into what matters
- generating fast prototypes so we can test an idea today, not “someday”

But here’s the part people miss:
I’m not “agent-vibe-coding” production.

Agents help me run quick tests, explore a solution space, and move faster.
Then I slow down, take control, and bring things up to standard.

Context is the real unlock.
We keep a `/docs` folder that acts like the company’s memory: what we decided, why we decided it, how we talk, what we ship, what we don’t.
When we use AI, we feed it that context so it behaves like a disciplined team member — not a random autocomplete machine.

One principle we try to follow:
AI can accelerate the work, but it can’t own the outcome.

If you’re building in travel and you’re using AI internally, I’d love to compare notes. What’s one workflow where it actually helped (not just demo-ed well)?

### Post 5 — We build fast. We also delete fast.
One thing I’ve learned building Wingle: speed is not only shipping.
Speed is also removing what doesn’t work.

We’ve started countless projects.
Some lasted weeks.
Some lasted days.
Some were genuinely good ideas… that simply didn’t survive contact with reality.

At the beginning, killing projects was painful.
You feel like you’re throwing away effort (and ego) along with the code.

Then we understood: this is the way.
If you want to find what really works, you have to be willing to trash what doesn’t — fast.

Our internal loop looks roughly like:
Build → test → measure → keep / discard → repeat.

The hard part is not building a feature.
The hard part is admitting:
“this is cool, but it’s not solving the problem strongly enough.”

We’ve done this across B2C and B2B:
- when something didn’t improve traveler clarity, it didn’t survive
- when something didn’t fit real agency workflows, it didn’t survive

It’s not romantic. But it’s honest.

Curious: in your product, what’s the last thing you removed that made it better?

### Post 6 — Same mission, two audiences (and why that matters)
Wingle has one mission: make travel better.

But we build for two audiences.
And that’s intentional.

Because travelers and travel professionals are dealing with the same problems from different angles:
- airports are high-stakes and unpredictable
- information is often unclear at the exact moment you need it
- premium experiences fail when coordination fails

So we build:
- for travelers: an app that upgrades the journey without subscriptions (credits when you need them)
- for travel professionals: two tools — one for premium airport experiences, one for itineraries + knowledge reuse

If you’re a travel professional: what’s one “small” airport moment that makes or breaks your client’s perception of the whole trip?

### Post 7 — A practical definition of “curation”
We use the word “curated” a lot in travel. But the word is cheap.

So here’s how I think about it when building products:

A curated experience is not “more options”.
It’s fewer options, backed by a quality bar, with clear next steps.

In premium airport services, that matters because “technically available” doesn’t mean “actually good”.

And in itineraries, it matters because more text doesn’t mean more clarity.

If you’re a travel professional and you want to pressure-test your current process:
- where are you still forced to “trust” without proof?
- where do you still send something to a traveler and hope they’ll understand it?

DM me if you want to compare notes (I’m collecting patterns).

### Post 8 — The part of travel tech that’s not software
There’s a part of travel tech that doesn’t fit neatly in a product screenshot:
coordination.

Airports are variable. Providers are variable. Timing is variable.

So a lot of the value is in how you handle variability:
- how you confirm what’s possible
- how you communicate it clearly
- how you keep the traveler calm when the environment is not

This is why we’re obsessed with “clear steps, no guesswork.”

If you’re building in travel: what’s your favorite example of a product that feels calm even when the system behind it is complex?

### Post 9 — The “/docs” folder is our unfair advantage (context > prompts)
I’ve learned this the hard way:
when you’re building fast, context becomes the bottleneck.

Not code. Not design. Context.

So we treat our `/docs` folder like a product:
- what Wingle is (and what it isn’t)
- how we talk to travelers vs travel professionals
- what we promise (and what we never promise)
- what we’re building, what we’re not, and why

It keeps humans aligned.
And honestly, it’s what makes AI useful too.

Without context, AI gives you “a” solution.
With context, it gives you your solution — closer to your standards, your constraints, your voice.

If you’re using AI in your company: where does your “source of truth” live today?
Is it in people’s heads, or in something your team (and your tools) can actually use?

### Post 10 — My rule for AI: prototype fast, production slow
I love speed.
But I also like control.

So this is my rule with AI:

- Prototype fast with agents (to test ideas, explore, build rough versions).
- Production slow with standards (refactor, simplify, remove, make it maintainable).

The danger is shipping the prototype.
It works… until it doesn’t.

When we ship something in Wingle (especially for travel professionals), it has to feel calm:
clear steps, no guesswork, no weird edge cases, no overpromises.

AI can help you get to a first version faster.
But the last mile is still a craft.

If you’ve shipped AI-assisted code to production: what’s the part you *always* rework manually after?

### Post 11 — “Which model do you use?” (the honest answer)
I get this question a lot: “Which model do you use?”

The honest answer is: I don’t use one.

I use different tools/models depending on the job:
- Cursor when I need codebase awareness and fast iteration
- Claude Code / Codex / ChatGPT depending on whether I’m exploring, drafting, or debugging
- and multiple models through Cursor when I want different perspectives quickly

But here’s what matters more than the model:
the context.

If I feed an assistant a random prompt, I get a random result.
If I feed it our `/docs` (what we’re building, what we won’t build, our tone, our constraints), I get something that feels like it came from our team.

So yeah — model choice matters.
But context + standards matter more.

Curious: do you have a real “source of truth” that your team (and your AI tools) can actually use?

### Post 12 — Product evolution: build fast, test fast, discard fast (without the drama)
Product evolution doesn’t have to be a dramatic “pivot story”.
Most of the time it’s just disciplined iteration:

Build fast → test in the real world → discard what doesn’t move the needle → keep what makes the experience calmer.

We’ve done this at Wingle across B2C and B2B.
If a feature is “cool” but doesn’t improve clarity for travelers (or doesn’t fit real agency workflows), it shouldn’t survive.

The tricky part is ego.
You don’t want to delete something you built.

But deleting is also product work.
Sometimes it’s the highest leverage product work.

If you’re building products: what’s one thing you stopped doing that immediately made the product better?
